Boating Guide

Planning your time on the River Dart

A practical introduction to the River Dart for leisure boaters, boat hirers, and families visiting the estuary. Not a substitute for official charts, local knowledge, or harbour authority guidance.

The River Dart — an overview

The River Dart runs roughly 46 miles from Dartmoor to the sea at Dartmouth, though the navigable tidal estuary begins in earnest around Totnes and stretches south through Stoke Gabriel, Dittisham, and Dartmouth before meeting the English Channel between Dartmouth Castle and Kingswear Castle.

This lower section — from Dartmouth Harbour to Totnes — is tidal, busy with river traffic in summer, and strikingly beautiful. It's one of the most popular leisure waterways in the South West, and one of the most enjoyable to explore at a relaxed pace.

The character of the river changes considerably along its length. The lower estuary near Dartmouth is relatively wide and exposed to wind funnelling up from the Channel. The middle reaches — around Dittisham and Greenway — feel sheltered and wooded. The upper river above Stoke Gabriel becomes progressively shallower and more tidal, requiring attention to both depth and the state of the tide.

Key locations on the Dart

Dartmouth is the main harbour town and the most convenient starting point for most visitors. The Boat Float in the town centre offers visitor moorings, and the Dart Marina and various hire operators are based here. The harbour entrance — between the twin castles — is the busiest part of the river and requires awareness of ferry crossings, vessel movements, and tidal flow.

Kingswear sits on the east bank opposite Dartmouth, connected by the Higher and Lower Ferries. Several boat hire operators and yacht charter companies are based here. The lower Dart between Dartmouth and Kingswear is a natural deep-water anchorage used by yachts waiting for a weather window or tide.

Dittisham, a few miles upstream from Dartmouth, is one of the most popular destinations on the river. The village has a small quay and pontoon, a pub popular with boaters, and swinging moorings in the pool. The stretch of river here is scenic and relatively sheltered. Passenger ferries run to Greenway — the former home of Agatha Christie — from nearby.

Stoke Gabriel is set in a tidal creek off the main river, accessible on a rising tide. The village is quiet and attractive, with a small quay and limited visitor moorings. Depths in the creek should be checked carefully before entering.

Totnes marks the practical head of navigation. A town of character and independent spirit, it's reachable by motor launch or small boat on a good tide, though the upper river above Stoke Gabriel is shallow in places and passage timing must be planned carefully around the tide.

Understanding wind on the Dart

Wind is the most significant variable for day-to-day boating on the Dart. Because the lower estuary runs roughly north–south, south-westerly winds — the prevailing direction in Devon — can funnel up the river and feel stronger than they appear on a forecast. A 12-knot south-westerly on the open sea can produce noticeably choppier conditions on the lower Dart, particularly near the harbour entrance and in the open section between Dartmouth and Dittisham.

As a rough guide, winds of up to 10–12 knots are generally comfortable for most hire boats and leisure craft on the river. Above 15–18 knots, the river can become uncomfortable, particularly on the lower and more exposed sections. Above 20 knots, less experienced crews should consider waiting for the wind to ease before heading out.

Wind direction also affects which parts of the river are more exposed. A northerly wind can create rough conditions near the harbour entrance and on the open reach between Dartmouth and Dittisham. An easterly — relatively uncommon in this part of Devon — can funnel through the valley and surprise crews unfamiliar with the area.

Tides and planning your passage

The River Dart is tidal throughout the navigable section, and tides significantly affect passage planning — particularly for slower craft and those venturing above Dittisham or Stoke Gabriel.

Tidal ranges in the Dart can be substantial, with spring tides producing a range of around 4.5–5 metres at Dartmouth. Tidal streams in the lower estuary can reach 2–3 knots at springs, particularly near the harbour entrance and in the narrows. At neap tides the streams are gentler.

Always check official tide times before heading out. DartBuddy links to ADMIRALTY EasyTide for the River Dart — the most reliable source for tide predictions in this area. Never rely on approximated or unofficial tide information for passage planning.

For most leisure trips — a day run from Dartmouth to Dittisham and back, for example — a basic understanding of the tide direction is sufficient. Aim to travel upriver on a flooding tide and return on an ebb. This makes for an easier, more comfortable trip and reduces fuel or effort if you're in a hire boat or on oars.

For trips further upriver — towards Stoke Gabriel or Totnes — careful timing is essential. These upper sections shoal significantly on a low tide, and some stretches are impassable to all but very shallow-draught craft in the lower half of the tidal cycle.

Beginner tips for hire boats and newcomers

  • Pick up a briefing from your hire operator before leaving — they will know current conditions, any local hazards, and where you can and can't go in the boat you're hiring.
  • Check the weather and wind forecast before you depart, and again on the morning of your trip. Conditions can change through the day.
  • Get the tide times and understand whether the tide is coming in or going out when you plan to depart. A quick check on ADMIRALTY EasyTide takes less than two minutes.
  • Keep clear of the passenger ferries operating between Dartmouth, Kingswear, and Dittisham. They run to a timetable and have limited ability to manoeuvre in the channel.
  • Stay well clear of the harbour entrance when large vessels — including the Dartmouth–Kingswear car ferry — are using it.
  • The river has speed limits in force in various sections. Hire operators will advise, but as a general rule proceed at a speed that creates minimal wash in the vicinity of moorings, other craft, and the riverbank.
  • Always carry a charged phone, and let someone ashore know your plans and expected return time.

Safety & emergency contacts

The RNLI Lifeboat Station at Torbay covers the mouth of the Dart and broader Torbay area. In an emergency at sea or on the tidal river, call the HM Coastguard on Channel 16 VHF or dial 999 and ask for the Coastguard.

Dartmouth Harbour Office can be reached on VHF Channel 11 for non-emergency queries about the harbour and river.

DartBuddy is a planning tool, not a navigation aid. Always use official charts, consult your hire operator, and take advice from the local harbour authority if you are unsure about conditions, depths, or passage planning on the Dart.
